Nothing Ear (3) Review: Great Sound, Solid ANC, and a "Super Mic" That Doesn't Quite Live Up to the Hype

Independently tested noise cancellation and sound quality that punch above the price point — but the headline feature falls short of its marketing promise.

Quick Verdict

The Nothing Ear (3) is Nothing's fourth-generation flagship earbud (the naming skips a number because the previous model was simply called "Nothing Ear"). Independent testing from SoundGuys found the Ear (3)'s ANC reduces the loudness of most sounds by around 82%, which is 7% more than the Nothing Ear (2024) average, placing these close to some of the best noise-canceling earbuds in that outlet's test pool. Sound quality also tested well: SoundGuys' panel-based scoring gave the Ear (3) an overall score of 4.7, in the high range for wireless earbuds in this price range.

The headline new feature — a "Super Mic" built into the charging case for calls and voice notes — is the part most reviewers found underwhelming. SoundGuys summarized it plainly: "the 'Super Mic' doesn't sound so super to my ears. There is less clipping with the Super Mic, but the voice quality sounds as muffled and robotic as the earbuds."

Amazon customer rating: Reported figures have varied over time as more reviews accumulate — Slickdeals listings show 4.2 stars from 66 reviews in October 2025 and 4.4 stars from 239 reviews by December 2025, and SoundGuys' recent deals post cites a 4.4-star rating. What Makes This Product Different?

The Ear (3)'s standout idea is the Super Mic: a pair of beamforming microphones built into the charging case itself, activated by a dedicated "Talk" button. According to 9to5Google's launch coverage, when you have the earbuds in use, the case can act as an alternate microphone for calls, voice notes, or using an assistant like Gemini, and pressing the Talk button while on a call switches from the earbuds to the case, which you hold close to your mouth.

That's paired with the brand's recognizable transparent design, now with metal accents and a heavier, more premium-feeling case, plus the personalized hearing-test EQ and Advanced Equalizer tools Nothing has offered since earlier Ear generations.

Main Features

Super Mic

Dual-microphone beamforming array in the charging case

What it is: A dual-microphone beamforming array embedded in the charging case, meant to serve as a secondary, higher-quality microphone for calls and voice notes.

How it works: Press and hold (or double-tap to lock) the Talk button on the case while holding it near your mouth; the case's mics take over from the earbuds' onboard mics.

Why it matters: In noisy environments, a mic positioned closer to your mouth can meaningfully improve how clearly your voice comes through, in principle.

Limitations: Compatibility is inconsistent. 9to5Google noted Nothing says the feature works with phone calls and "most major apps" such as Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and Google Meet, as well as WhatsApp and WeChat, and some apps like Blackmagic Camera and Voice Memos on iOS, but voice messages in apps, recording apps, and even the phone's camera app might not be able to use that mic at all. Multiple outlets described the resulting voice quality as underwhelming relative to the marketing.

Adaptive ANC

Hybrid active noise cancellation rated at up to 45dB

What it is: Hybrid active noise cancellation rated by Nothing at up to 45dB.

How it works: According to GSMArena's hands-on report, the system adjusts according to your surroundings at a 600 ms pace.

Why it matters: Independent lab data from SoundGuys is the most reliable evidence here, showing real-world attenuation of up to 35dB in low frequencies and up to 38dB in high frequencies, reducing overall loudness by around 82% — genuinely competitive for the price, even though it falls short of the manufacturer's 45dB figure.

Limitations: SoundGuys also noted the ANC system struggled with even a slight amount of wind while walking outdoors, which may matter more or less depending on where you typically use them.

Sound Quality and Personalization

12mm ceramic driver with personalized EQ

What it is: A 12mm dynamic driver with a bass-forward "V-shaped" default tuning, plus an in-app hearing test and Advanced Equalizer.

How it works: The Nothing X app runs a short hearing test and applies personalized EQ; users can also manually adjust frequency bands.

Why it matters: SoundGuys' panel testing rated the Ear (3) highly across its Multi-Dimensional Audio Quality Score, noting low distortion, an impressive result, and an overall score of 4.7, in the high range for wireless earbuds in this price range. TechRadar's reviewer similarly found the sound engaging, if a shade off excellent for dynamic nuance and treble clarity.

Limitations: The default tuning is bass-heavy and won't suit listeners who prefer flat or treble-forward sound without using the EQ tools.

Battery Life

Up to 5.5 hours with ANC, 38 hours total with case

What it is: Manufacturer-rated battery life of up to 5.5 hours on the earbuds with ANC on, up to 10 hours with ANC off, and up to 38 hours total with the case.

Why it matters: 9to5Google reported this is an improvement over the prior model, with the earbuds getting a slightly bigger 55 mAh battery, leading to 5.5 hours of ANC-enabled battery life, up from 5.2 hours on Nothing Ear.

Limitations: TechRadar's reviewer called battery life their biggest gripe with the product, and GSMArena's review noted battery life is just okay, with more available elsewhere among flagship competitors.

Real-World Experience and Common Feedback Patterns

Reddit discussion aggregated by RedditRecs showed a mostly positive reception, with one recurring theme: the ANC is a clear step up from the prior generation but still trails Apple's AirPods Pro. One frequently cited comment put it this way: "The ear (3) has a much improved ANC system according to reviewers and its ANC algorithm is quite refined now but it's not nearly as good as AirPods Pro 2." The same source noted that owners of the Super Mic feature find it works well when the case is held close to the mouth while talking, using the "Talk" button.

This is user-reported feedback rather than independent lab data, and should be read as a pattern rather than a confirmed technical fact.

On the Super Mic specifically, the negative pattern is far more consistent across independent reviewers than it is among casual users — multiple technology publications (SoundGuys, Gizmodo, 9to5Google, Reviews.org, How-To Geek) independently arrived at similar conclusions after hands-on testing, which strengthens the case that this is a genuine, recurring limitation rather than an isolated complaint.

Problems & Limitations

  • Super Mic inconsistency: This is the most consistently reported issue. Reviews.org described the execution as a little too awkward to use, with results that rarely feel worth the trouble. 9to5Google's hands-on test found that when music played loudly nearby, switching to Super Mic meant a call partner could hear the background music while the speaker talked, when normal earbud mics had blocked it out entirely.
  • Limited app support for the Super Mic beyond calls and a small set of native apps.
  • Shorter ANC-on battery life compared with some rivals, per TechRadar and GSMArena.
  • Case shows wear easily, according to How-To Geek's hands-on notes on the transparent panel.
  • Default sound signature may not suit all listeners without manual EQ adjustment.

Where competitors may do better: Apple's AirPods Pro and Sony's flagship earbuds are generally regarded by reviewers (TechRadar, GSMArena) as offering better ANC and, in Apple's case, deeper ecosystem integration, though usually at a similar or higher price.

Who Should Buy It?

Android users who want strong sound and above-average ANC without paying flagship Sony/Bose/Apple prices.

Buyers who enjoy tinkering with EQ and personalized sound profiles.

People drawn to Nothing's distinctive transparent design language who want a premium-feeling case.

Who Should Not Buy It?

Anyone buying primarily for the Super Mic feature as a serious microphone replacement — independent testing suggests it underdelivers relative to the marketing.

Buyers who need long ANC-on battery life for all-day wear.

iPhone-first users who want the deepest ecosystem integration (Apple's own earbuds will generally serve that need better).

Value for Money

At its $179 list price, the Ear (3) sits in a competitive but crowded segment. Independent ANC and sound-quality testing from SoundGuys suggests the core listening experience punches above the price point. However, the Super Mic — the feature most differentiating this generation from its predecessor and from competitors — has been consistently described by independent reviewers as underdelivering. Multiple deal-tracking sources show the Ear (3) regularly discounted into the roughly $120–$170 range, which meaningfully improves the value proposition, since you're paying less for a feature (Super Mic) that reviewers broadly agree isn't fully realized yet. As with any Amazon listing, this range shifts over time, so it's worth checking the current price before deciding.

Final Verdict

The Nothing Ear (3) delivers on the fundamentals: independently tested ANC and sound quality that are genuinely competitive for a sub-$200 pair of earbuds, wrapped in a distinctive, well-built design. The Super Mic is the product's most ambitious idea and its clearest shortfall — nearly every independent outlet that tested it found the execution didn't match the pitch.

If you're buying for everyday listening, commuting, and calls through the standard earbud mics, the Ear (3) is a solid, well-reviewed option, especially at a discounted price. If the Super Mic is your main reason for choosing this model over the cheaper Ear (3a) or a rival flagship, it's worth tempering expectations based on the consistent independent feedback above.
