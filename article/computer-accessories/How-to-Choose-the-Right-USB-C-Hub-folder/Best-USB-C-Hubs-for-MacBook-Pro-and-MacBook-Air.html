Quick Verdict

There is no single USB-C hub that is right for every MacBook.

The right choice depends mainly on your MacBook model, number of displays, required USB speeds, card-reader needs, Ethernet requirements, and charging expectations.

For a simple MacBook Air or entry-level MacBook setup, a compact USB-C hub can add HDMI, USB-A, Ethernet, SD cards, and charging without the cost or size of a full desktop dock.

For a permanent workstation with multiple high-bandwidth peripherals, a Thunderbolt dock makes more sense.

The important distinction is not simply how many ports a product has. USB-C hubs and Thunderbolt docks can look similar while offering very different bandwidth, display, and expansion capabilities.

Why MacBook Users Need a Hub

Apple's current MacBook designs keep the number of physical ports relatively limited compared with many desktop PCs.

The current MacBook Air uses two Thunderbolt 4/USB-C ports, alongside MagSafe 3 and a headphone jack. Apple's current specifications also allow the supported Air configuration to drive up to two external displays, depending on the model and display configuration.

The current MacBook Pro lineup varies much more significantly by chip.

The M5 MacBook Pro uses Thunderbolt 4 ports, while M5 Pro and M5 Max configurations use three Thunderbolt 5 ports. Apple lists substantially different external-display capabilities between these configurations. M5 supports up to two external displays, M5 Pro up to three, and M5 Max up to four, subject to Apple's specified resolution and refresh-rate combinations.

That distinction matters when shopping for a hub.

For example, buying a hub with two HDMI outputs does not automatically mean that your MacBook can independently drive two external displays through those outputs. The MacBook itself, the hub's display architecture, macOS, and the connection standard all matter.

A hub is therefore less about "adding random USB ports" and more about solving a specific connectivity problem:

  • External monitor
  • Keyboard and mouse
  • Ethernet
  • SD or microSD cards
  • External SSD
  • USB-A accessories
  • Charging
  • Multiple displays

USB-C Hub vs. Thunderbolt Dock: What Is the Difference?

This is the most important thing to understand before buying.

USB-C is a connector, not a single speed

"USB-C" describes the physical connector. It does not tell you whether a device supports 5Gbps, 10Gbps, 20Gbps, USB4, Thunderbolt, display output, or a particular charging rate.

A USB-C hub can therefore be very basic or surprisingly capable.

Always check the actual specification for:

  • USB generation
  • Maximum data rate
  • Display protocol
  • Display resolution and refresh rate
  • Power input
  • Power delivered to the Mac
  • Ethernet speed
  • Card-reader speed

USB-C hubs

A conventional USB-C hub is usually designed around portability and a relatively low price.

Depending on the model, it may provide:

  • HDMI or DisplayPort
  • USB-A
  • USB-C data
  • SD/microSD
  • Ethernet
  • USB-C power input

This is generally enough for a laptop user who needs one display and several ordinary peripherals.

The UGREEN Revodok 6-in-1, for example, advertises DisplayPort up to 4K/60Hz, 10Gbps data on several ports, and up to 85W of laptop charging from a 100W PD input.

Thunderbolt docks

Thunderbolt docks are a different category.

Thunderbolt 4 provides up to 40Gbps of bandwidth, while Thunderbolt 5 increases the headline bandwidth substantially and supports newer display and connectivity capabilities.

The important point is not simply that "40Gbps is faster."

Thunderbolt is designed to provide a high-bandwidth connection between the computer and the dock, making it much better suited to workflows involving combinations of:

  • Multiple displays
  • Fast external SSDs
  • High-speed networking
  • Multiple USB devices
  • Professional storage
  • Permanent desktop setups

However, a Thunderbolt dock does not override the display limitations of your MacBook.

Your Mac's chip and Apple-supported display configuration still determine what displays can actually be driven.

Before You Buy: Five Specifications That Actually Matter

1. Check your exact MacBook model first

Do not buy a hub based only on the words "MacBook Pro" or "MacBook Air."

The display capabilities of an M5 MacBook Pro are different from an M5 Pro or M5 Max MacBook Pro.

Likewise, different MacBook Air generations have different external-display capabilities.

Before buying, identify:

  1. MacBook model
  2. Chip
  3. Number of external displays required
  4. Desired resolution
  5. Desired refresh rate

Apple's own technical specifications should be your starting point for display compatibility.

2. Do not assume "4K@60Hz" works the same on Mac and Windows

A product listing may advertise a maximum display resolution that does not apply identically to every operating system.

Satechi's Multiport Adapter V3 is a particularly useful example.

Macworld's testing found that the adapter can support 8K/30Hz on Windows, while the same hardware is limited to 4K/60Hz on a Mac, with additional supported combinations such as 2K/144Hz and 1080p/240Hz depending on the Mac. Macworld updated its review to reflect this limitation.

That is why the important question is not:

"Does this hub support 8K?"

The better question is:

"What resolution and refresh rate does this hub support on my exact MacBook?"

3. Look at actual charging output

"100W PD" does not necessarily mean your MacBook receives 100W.

The 100W figure can describe the hub's maximum power input.

The hub itself consumes some power, so the amount available to the Mac can be lower.

For example, Anker specifies that the 552 accepts up to 100W through its PD input but provides up to 85W to the connected laptop after accounting for the hub's own power consumption.

The same principle applies to the Anker 332, which accepts 100W but specifies up to 85W laptop output.

Therefore:

100W input ≠ 100W laptop charging.

4. Think about where the hub will live

A small travel hub and a desktop dock solve different problems.

Travel hub

Best when you:

  • Carry your MacBook every day
  • Move between offices or classrooms
  • Occasionally connect a monitor
  • Need SD cards or USB-A
  • Want something that fits inside a laptop bag

Desktop dock

Best when you:

  • Keep your MacBook on the same desk
  • Connect several peripherals
  • Use multiple displays
  • Use fast external storage
  • Want one cable to connect the laptop to your workstation

5. Don't pay for ports you won't use

An 18-port dock sounds impressive.

But if you only need:

  • One monitor
  • Keyboard
  • Mouse
  • Charging

you probably don't need an 18-port Thunderbolt dock.

A smaller hub may give you the same practical result for much less money.

USB-C Hub vs. Thunderbolt Dock: Which One Do You Need?

Choose a basic USB-C hub if:

  • You use one external monitor.
  • You need a few USB accessories.
  • You occasionally use Ethernet.
  • You need an SD card reader.
  • You want something portable.
  • You do not regularly transfer data at Thunderbolt-level speeds.

Examples: Anker 552, UGREEN Revodok 6-in-1, Anker 332

Choose a Thunderbolt dock if:

  • You use multiple displays.
  • You regularly connect fast external SSDs.
  • You have a permanent workstation.
  • You need high-speed networking.
  • You want many peripherals connected through one cable.
  • Your MacBook supports the display configuration you need.

Examples: CalDigit TS4, UGREEN Revodok Max 213

Choose a USB4 travel hub if:

  • You travel frequently.
  • You want higher-speed connectivity than a basic 5Gbps hub.
  • You want a compact accessory.
  • You need a mixture of display, networking, and card-reader connections.

Example: Satechi Pro Hub Max

Common Problems With USB-C Hubs

1. Display flickering

A flickering display does not automatically mean the hub is defective. Possible causes include:

  • HDMI cable limitations
  • Incorrect resolution or refresh-rate settings
  • Hub bandwidth limitations
  • Insufficient power
  • Host-port limitations
  • Display compatibility
  • macOS configuration

Before replacing the hub, test:

  1. Another HDMI/DisplayPort cable.
  2. A lower refresh rate.
  3. A lower resolution.
  4. Direct connection from the MacBook to the display.
  5. The same hub with another computer, if available.

This helps identify whether the problem is the Mac, cable, display, or hub.

2. A hub may become warm

Compact hubs can become warm during charging or high-speed data transfers. Anker's own support documentation states that increased temperature during charging or high-speed transfers can be normal for its hubs and recommends not covering the device. Warm does not automatically mean defective. However, unusual behavior such as repeated disconnects, crashes, or loss of ports should be investigated separately.

3. The hub's "100W" number can be misleading

This is one of the most common specification misunderstandings. For example: 100W PD input → hub consumes power → up to 85W reaches the laptop. That is exactly how Anker describes the 552 and 332. Always look for the specification labeled: PD output to host / laptop charging output rather than assuming the largest wattage on the product page is what reaches the MacBook.

4. More HDMI ports do not automatically mean more Mac displays

This deserves repeating. A hub may physically contain two HDMI ports. That does not automatically mean: MacBook + hub = two independent displays. The Mac's own display architecture matters. For example, Apple's current M5, M5 Pro, and M5 Max MacBook Pro configurations support different numbers of external displays. Therefore, check both: MacBook display support + hub display architecture before buying.

5. A USB-C port is not necessarily a display port

The presence of a USB-C connector does not mean that it supports video. Anker explicitly states that the USB-C data port on the 332 does not support display output. This is another reason to check the individual port specification instead of judging by the connector shape.

What About Older Intel MacBooks?

Many USB-C and Thunderbolt accessories remain compatible with older Macs, but compatibility should be checked model by model. Older Intel MacBooks can have different:

  • Display limitations
  • Thunderbolt generations
  • Charging capabilities
  • macOS support
  • External-display configurations

Therefore, do not assume that a hub's current Apple silicon performance will be identical on an older Intel Mac. If you own an Intel MacBook, check the Mac's original technical specifications alongside the hub manufacturer's compatibility list.

What We Would Check Before You Buy

Before clicking the purchase button, verify these seven things:

  1. Your exact MacBook model - Do not stop at "MacBook Pro." Check the chip and generation.
  2. Display requirement - Ask: One or two displays? 1080p, 1440p, 4K, 5K, or 6K? 60Hz, 120Hz, 144Hz, or higher?
  3. Connection type - Do you need: HDMI? DisplayPort? USB4? Thunderbolt?
  4. Charging output - Look for the actual laptop output, not just the PD input rating.
  5. Storage requirements - If you use an external SSD, check the actual USB/Thunderbolt speed of the port you will connect it to.
  6. Card-reader requirements - Photographers should check: SD vs microSD, UHS-I vs UHS-II, Reader speed.
  7. Ethernet speed - Gigabit Ethernet is sufficient for many users. 2.5GbE becomes more useful if your local network, NAS, router, and internet connection can actually take advantage of it.

Final Verdict

There is no universal "best" USB-C hub for every MacBook. The right choice is determined primarily by your MacBook's capabilities and the devices you need to connect.

For a typical setup involving one monitor, USB accessories, Ethernet, SD cards, and laptop charging, the Anker 552 is a practical compact option, but its 4K HDMI output is limited to 30Hz and its 100W PD input translates to up to 85W for the laptop.

For a permanent workstation with many peripherals, the CalDigit TS4 provides a much larger Thunderbolt 4 expansion platform, including 18 ports and up to 98W host charging. Its lack of HDMI is an important consideration for some monitor setups.

For users specifically interested in a Thunderbolt 4 desktop setup with dual 4K/60Hz display support on macOS, the UGREEN Revodok Max 213 is worth considering, provided its current specifications match your MacBook and monitor configuration. UGREEN explicitly distinguishes its macOS dual-4K capability from its Windows 8K capability.

For travel, the Satechi Pro Hub Max combines USB4, HDMI, Ethernet, SD/microSD, and up to 96W pass-through charging in a compact design intended for compatible MacBook Pro models.

For a simple, low-cost monitor-and-accessories setup, the UGREEN Revodok 6-in-1 provides DisplayPort up to 4K/60Hz and up to 85W laptop output from its 100W PD input.

And if photography or video work is your priority, the Satechi Multiport V3 offers a useful combination of SD, Ethernet, multiple USB-C data ports, HDMI, and 85W pass-through — with the important caveat that its 8K headline applies to Windows, while Mac support tops out at 4K/60Hz under the tested configuration.

The most important buying lesson is simple: Do not buy a USB-C hub based only on its port count or the largest number printed on the box. Check your exact MacBook model, the number and type of displays you need, the actual charging output, the data speeds of the ports you will use, and whether the advertised display capability applies to macOS. And because prices and product configurations can change, verify the current retailer listing before purchasing.
